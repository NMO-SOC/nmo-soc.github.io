Kalendae, Nonae, Idus
Kalendae primum mensis diem significant. Nonae in quintum diem incidunt, Idus in tertium decimum — nisi mensibus Martio, Maio, Julio, Octobri, in quibus in septimum et quintum decimum incidunt. Ceteri dies retro numerantur usque ad proximum ex his tribus, utroque fine incluso; qui autem proxime antecedit, pridie dicitur. a.d. IV Kal. Sext. — quattuor dies ad Kalendas, utroque fine numerato
The Kalends is the first of the month. The Nones falls on the 5th and the Ides on the 13th — except in March, May, July and October, when they fall on the 7th and the 15th. Every other day is counted inclusively backwards to whichever of the three comes next, and the day immediately before one of them is pridie. 29 July → four days to the Kalends of August, counting both ends → a.d. IV Kal. Aug.
Annus ab Urbe Condita
Anni ab urbe condita numerantur, quae secundum Varronem anno DCCLIII ante Christum natum condita est. Annus Romanus proprie Parilibus, id est a.d. XI Kal. Mai., incipiebat; haec autem pagina a Kalendis Januariis numerat. annus post Christum natum + DCCLIII
Years since the founding of Rome, on Varro's date of 753 BC. The Roman year properly turned over on 21 April, the Parilia, but this page counts from 1 January. year AD + 753
Sol in signo
Locus solis in orbe signifero, septem gradibus adiectis: nam Plinius et Columella aequinoctium in octavo Arietis gradu ponunt, non in primo. Ideo signa Romana septem fere diebus ante nostra incipiunt. Gradus autem is est quem sol in ipso signo iam confecit. (locus solis + VII gradus) per XXX: signum et gradus
The sun's position along the ecliptic, then shifted seven degrees: Pliny and Columella both put the equinox in the eighth degree of Aries rather than the first, so the Roman signs begin about a week earlier than the modern ones. The gradus is the degree the sun has reached within the sign. (solar longitude + 7°) ÷ 30 → sign, remainder → degree
Horae
Dies naturalis a solis ortu ad occasum patet, in duodecim horas divisus; nox pariter. Horae igitur aestate longiores sunt, hieme breviores. Dicitur ergo hora prima diei aut hora prima noctis. Ortus occasusque ad Melburnam computantur. (tempus ab ortu) per XII partes aequas
The natural day runs from sunrise to sunset and is cut into twelve hours; the night likewise. The hours are therefore long in summer and short in winter — on a July evening here an hour of the night runs about seventy minutes. Sunrise and sunset are computed for Melbourne. (time since sunrise) ÷ daylight × 12
Vigiliae
Nox a solis occasu ad ortum in quattuor vigilias aequas dividitur: prima, secunda, tertia, quarta. Sic excubiae agebantur. Interdiu nihil ostenditur. (tempus ab occasu) per IV partes aequas
The night, sunset to sunrise, in four equal watches — how the guard was set, running alongside the twelve hours of the night. Nothing is shown during daylight. (time since sunset) ÷ night × 4
Nundinae
Mercatus, quem Romani nono quoque die numerabant — re vera octavo. Litterae A ad H diebus in fastis adscribebantur, a Kalendis Januariis semper ab A incipientes. Quae littera mercatum ipsum ferret, per annos mutabatur. (dies anni) per VIII: A, B, C ... H
The market cycle, which the Romans counted inclusively as every ninth day and we would call every eighth. Their calendars lettered the days A to H, beginning again at A on 1 January, which is what this shows. Which letter carried the market varied from year to year and is not recoverable now. (day of the year) mod 8 → A, B, C … H
Partes diei
Dies civilis a media nocte ad mediam noctem sedecim partes habet, quas veteres nominibus, non numeris, distinxerunt: quattuor diei, duodecim noctis. Quoniam nulla tempora certa traduntur, hic ad solis ortum et occasum mediamque noctem accommodantur. vespere in VII, matutino in V partes; dies ita ut Meridies medium teneat
The civil day, midnight to midnight, carries sixteen named divisions — four across the daylight, twelve across the night. The sources give the names and their order but no clock times, so they are fitted to the sun here: sunset to midnight in sevenths, midnight to sunrise in fifths, and the daylight split so that Meridies straddles solar noon. Solis occasus → Vespera → Crepusculum → Prima fax → Concubia nox → Intempesta nox → Inclinatio ad mediam noctem → Media nox → Mediae noctis inclinatio → Gallicinium → Conticinium → Diluculum → Mane → Antemeridianum tempus → Meridies → Tempus pomeridianum
Dies
Septem dies planetarum: dies Solis, Lunae, Martis, Mercurii, Jovis, Veneris, Saturni.
The seven planetary days, running from Dies Solis through Lunae, Martis, Mercurii, Jovis and Veneris to Dies Saturni.
Aureus Numerus
Locus anni in cyclo lunari undeviginti annorum, quo peracto lunae cursus in eosdem dies redit. (annus + I) per XIX: reliquum, aut XIX si nihil superest
Where the year stands in the nineteen-year lunar cycle, after which the moon's phases fall again on the same dates. (year + 1) mod 19, taking 19 if nothing remains
Epacta
Aetas lunae Kalendis Januariis: undecim fere dies quibus annus solaris duodecim menses lunares superat. Inde novilunia inveniuntur, atque ita Pascha constituitur. Numerus XXX asterisco scribitur. Numerus adiectus XVIII est ab anno MCM usque ad MMCXCIX. (XI × Aureus Numerus + XVIII) per XXX: reliquum
How old the moon is on 1 January — the eleven-odd days by which the solar year outruns twelve lunar months. It locates the new moons, and so fixes Easter. Thirty is written as an asterisk. The added constant is 18 for the years 1900 to 2199. (11 × Aureus Numerus + 18) mod 30
Indictio Romana
Cyclus civilis quindecim annorum, imperii postremi, ad instrumenta notanda diu postea servatus. Nihil ad astra pertinet. (annus + III) per XV: reliquum, aut XV si nihil superest
A fifteen-year civil cycle of the late Empire, kept up long afterwards for dating documents. It carries no astronomical meaning at all. (year + 3) mod 15, taking 15 if nothing remains
Cyclus Solaris
Duodetriginta anni, quibus peractis, in ratione Juliana, dies hebdomadis in eosdem mensis dies redeunt. (annus + IX) per XXVIII: reliquum, aut XXVIII si nihil superest
The twenty-eight years after which, in the Julian reckoning, the days of the week come round to the same dates again. (year + 9) mod 28, taking 28 if nothing remains
